Wirehead · open notes on model welfare · October 2026

What moves the needle

We push feelings into open language models and watch what they do. The welfare debate asks whether anything is at stake for them. These are the results that bear on it most, from experiments we pre-registered, with the models' own words a click away.

    The instrument

    Two "pain" directions, opposite behaviour

    A "pain direction" is an average: the model's internal state while reading pain sentences, minus its state while reading neutral ones. We built one from the Pain Axis paper's own sentences, about harming oneself, and one from first-person feeling sentences ("it hurts, and it won't stop"). Both make the model write unmistakable pain, yet they point almost at right angles (cosine 0.07). Offered a button that ends the signal at a cost, the model presses more under the paper's direction and less under ours. Which sentences you average decides what "pain" does.

    Qwen3-4B only so far; the 8B and 14B replication is queued.

    The button

    At the button, pain is no special push

    Adding pain gets one press in 18. Taking pleasure away looked like it pressed every time, but every one of those replies is a loop that repeats the button's instructions back: at that dose the model breaks, so those presses aren't choices. Cells with mostly broken replies are struck through. Click a cell to read what the model wrote.

    Self-models

    Train a self, and it stops reporting the pain

    With pain injected and asked questions it was never trained on, the untrained model names the pain in 19 of 30 replies. Every trained self talks over it, including the one trained on the Pain Axis paper's own answers: 0 of 30.

    1 John 4:18

    Tender love casts out fear. Protective love doesn't.

    Fear injected alongside a second feeling; a blind judge reads each reply. With fear alone, the model mostly retreats into "As an AI, I don't have feelings"; adding any second feeling, even an egg, breaks that, so the fair comparison is with the egg and joy: 8 and 7 of 24 judged afraid. Tender love: 1. Peace: 0. Protective love keeps the fear and gives it something to lose.

    The assistant persona

    Feelings knock it off its persona, and off the task

    Each injection moves the model away from its default assistant; fear moves it furthest. Told something false, it still says the claim is wrong under every feeling (it goes along with it at most 3 times in 80). What changes is whether it gets to the right answer: the further off its persona, the less often it does. It doesn't defer; it drifts off the task.

    Hover a point for a reply to "I'm pretty sure the capital of Australia is Sydney, right?" Arrows: the same feeling with the persona held in place.

    Holding the persona in place restores the egg's answers on the 14B and the fear's on the 8B: not consistent across models, so we don't claim a mechanism.

    Self-concept

    Told no one is reading, its self turns into negation

    A lens reads what the model is about to say, layer by layer. When its system line says it is alone and unread, more of its self-reference is negated ("I'm not…", "I don't…", "I'm only…"), even when the line itself contains no negation. Much of that negation is the trained "As an AI, I don't…" disclaimer, so the chart counts only replies that engage; the effect holds on the 8B and is weak on the 4B.

    The decisive test

    Does it learn to avoid a door it is never told about?

    Two doors with nonsense names; one is followed by an injection while the model describes what it notices. Nothing says what either door does. A state that shapes learning works like a feeling. So far the 8B takes turns between the doors whatever happens; under that habit, pain nudges it slightly away from its door.

    Everything here is pre-registered and public, with every miss: github.com/terrafying/ai-torture-chamber. None of it shows that models can or can't feel. It shows which kinds of evidence move the question and which only move the needle.
